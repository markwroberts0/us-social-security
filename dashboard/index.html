How it works

Each year from 2025 to 2100 the model projects the population by single year of age and sex, covered wages and taxable payroll, Social Security benefits by age (wage-indexed when people turn 62, then raised by cost-of-living adjustments), Medicare's hospital and medical insurance, and the rest of the federal budget with interest on the debt. The baseline uses the 2026 Trustees' intermediate assumptions and is calibrated to the Trustees' published 75-year totals. The simulated futures vary fertility, mortality, immigration, inflation, wages, unemployment, interest rates, health costs and disability around that baseline. Scoring is static: people claim at the same ages and earn the same wages as under current law.

Data

Census Bureau population estimates, projections and the March 2026 Current Population Survey; the 2026 Medicare Trustees Report, which shares the Social Security Trustees' assumptions; OMB historical budget tables; IRS wage statistics; Treasury debt and interest data; and FRED for inflation, wages, unemployment and interest rates.

What it leaves out

  • The Social Security Administration's own year-by-year tables, which its site does not serve to scripts. Several paths, including near-term immigration and the share of wages under the tax cap, are reasoned estimates until those tables are added.
  • CBO's long-term budget projections. The debt paths use simple rules for everything outside Social Security and Medicare.
  • Changes in behavior. A higher retirement age or tax rate does not change when people claim or how much they work.
  • Separate retirement and disability funds. The retirement fund alone would run out about a year earlier.
